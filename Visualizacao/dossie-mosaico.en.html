The Case of the Mosaic of Uses

Between 2020 and 2024, a MapBiomas class meant for ambiguous pixels (cropland or pasture, indistinguishable at 30 metres) absorbed almost all of the recent agricultural expansion of Goiás, while the "Agriculture" class stood still. Four independent tests examine whether the change lies in the landscape or in the measuring yardstick.

Chapter 1

The correction that opened the case

The investigation grew out of a question about the age of pastures that are converted to cropland, which requires separating Pasture (class 15) from Agriculture (class 19) in the annual MapBiomas map. A third class, the "Mosaic of Uses" (class 21), is reserved for pixels where the satellite image (30 metres) cannot tell whether that spot is cropland, pasture or a mixture of the two. In the original count this class was left out because a line was missing from the lookup table, and an automatic fill covered the gap with the wrong answer, making the pixels vanish from the count without any error message.

The accounting defect

About 4,900 pixels whose previous class was Mosaic of Uses were being discarded as "unknown age", when their age was known and they simply did not fit the category table. Once the mapping was corrected, the volume of usable data rose by 44%, and the rescued pixels were disproportionately of mixed cropland/pasture origin.

Correcting the accounting would have closed the matter, but replacing the sample with a full census of every pixel in Goiás over the 40 years of the series revealed the pattern that the rest of this page examines.

"Unknown age" is legitimate, because age can only be computed when the previous conversion was also observed within the series; the relevant column is the one of recorded conversions, which plummets, and the median, which falls with it.

Catalogued conversions fall from over a million in 2019 to 157 thousand in 2024, and the median age falls from 22 to a range between 4 and 11 years. Four alternative explanations were examined, and none of them held up. Reclassification, in which new agriculture subclasses would be entering the count, does not hold because the total Agriculture area on the map is practically static, as Chapter 4 shows. Spatial concentration, in which the drop would come from fewer municipalities being observed, does not either, since the drop is statewide and monotonic across the whole territory. A change in farmer behaviour contradicts what the field data record, and Chapter 4 gives the numbers. That leaves an isolated hiccup in one specific version of the map, to which Chapter 8 is entirely devoted, and which is not confirmed either.

With all four ruled out, the remaining question was whether the measure we were taking, "pasture that became cropland", still meant in 2024 the same thing that it meant in 2015.

Chapter 3

The destination of pasture shifted from Agriculture to Mosaic

The earlier analysis asked only whether pasture became cropland. The full question covers every possible destination, measured pixel by pixel over the 379 million 30-metre cells of Goiás across the 40 years of the series. The cut that matters is the split of the pasture that leaves between Agriculture and Mosaic of Uses.

In 2015, the Mosaic/Agriculture ratio between these destinations was 0.60×, with more than half going to Agriculture. In 2024, 85.9% of pasture exits end up in Mosaic and only 2.6% in Agriculture, a ratio of 32.5×.

What this means

The original analysis, "pasture that became cropland", measures an ever smaller slice of the phenomenon. In 2024, the "Agriculture" class captures about 7.5% of the conversion volume it captured in 2015, not because conversion shrank in that proportion, but because the destination the map assigns to it changed.

Chapter 4

Soy grew 38% while the map's Agriculture stood still

The simplest reading of the previous chapter would be that conversion slowed down. Four anchors outside remote sensing show that this reading is false.

Planted soy — SIDRA/IBGE (measured in the field) Agriculture — MapBiomas Mosaic of Uses — MapBiomas Union (Agriculture + Mosaic)
Figure 2. Cumulative area in Goiás, 2015–2024, in millions of hectares (Mha), restricted to the state polygon. Field soy and the Mosaic rise almost side by side; the map's Agriculture barely moves.

Soy planted in Goiás, which IBGE surveys in the field, went from 3.578 to 4.942 million hectares between 2020 and 2024, +1.364 Mha (+38.1%). Over the same interval, MapBiomas "Agriculture" went from 5.668 to 5.732 Mha (+63,930 ha, +1.1%), against +150 to +180 thousand ha/year between 2015 and 2017. The Mosaic grew from 2.235 to 3.586 Mha (+1.351 Mha), a magnitude almost identical to IBGE's new soy. The coincidence is too large to be accidental, although it is not proof in itself, since it shows that the accounts balance, not that we know which pixels moved. Pasture keeps leaving, from 13.146 to 11.989 Mha (−1.157 Mha, −8.8%), without being counted as arriving in Agriculture.

There is real and accelerating agricultural expansion in Goiás in these years, visible in the field data, recorded by MapBiomas under the label "Mosaic of Uses", not "Agriculture".

Chapter 5

Two competing hypotheses

The growth of the Mosaic of Uses admits two readings that the previous chapters do not separate. The first (hypothesis A) is a behaviour of the classifier at the end of the series, since the technical documentation of Collection 10 states that the post-classification filters use backward-looking windows of three and four years, so that a conversion near the temporal edge of the map does not have enough future years to be confirmed, and the classifier may prefer the more cautious answer, "Mosaic". The second (hypothesis B) is a real change in the landscape, since crop-livestock integration is a growing practice in Goiás and a genuinely more mixed landscape should produce more Mosaic because it is right, not because it fails. The two hypotheses produce the same aggregate pattern, and the account balances of Chapter 4, though suggestive, do not show that they are the same pixels. Separating them requires evidence that treats each one distinctly, which is the subject of the four tests that follow.

Chapter 6 · Test 1

The shape of the signal points to the yardstick, not the field

If the cause were a real change in the field, the map's metric should follow, in shape, the smooth growth that IBGE measures in cropland. If it were a behaviour of the classifier, the metric would move in jolts.

Planted soy, annual change (field) Pasture→Agriculture, annual change (map)
Figure 3. Year-on-year percentage change. Field soy fluctuates within a narrow band and is almost always positive; the map's pasture→Agriculture flow plunges in ever steeper steps.

Field soy grows smoothly, with annual changes between −0.5% and +10.6%. The pasture→Agriculture flow moves in steps, at −37.2% in 2016, +11.5% in 2017 and −22.9% in 2018, and then descends in ever steeper drops, −37.9% in 2020 and −52.2% in 2021, the latter in the year that soy grew +7.8%.

Why the shape matters

The decisions of thousands of independent farmers tend to produce a smooth aggregate at state scale; a threshold shift in the classifier tends to produce steps. The shape of the signal favours the yardstick hypothesis, although an irregular diffusion of crop-livestock integration could also produce jumps.

Chapter 7 · Test 2

The growth of the Mosaic is concentrated on the frontier

The geographic centre of mass of each land-use variable in Goiás, which is the area-weighted balance point, shows the agricultural frontier marching north over four decades. If the map's Agriculture underestimates the recent expansion, its centre of mass should stall relative to sources independent of the classifier.

Agriculture (MapBiomas) Planted soy (SIDRA, field) Agriculture ∪ Mosaic (MapBiomas) Cattle herd (SIDRA, field)
Figure 4. Latitude of the centre of mass, 2018–2024 (degrees South; up = further north). Agriculture is the only line that does not move; adding the Mosaic restores the advance north.

Between 2019 and 2024, the centre of mass of Agriculture advanced only +0.2 km north (95% CI includes zero), while planted soy and the cattle herd (IBGE) advanced +8 to +12 km. The Agriculture+Mosaic union advances +4.1 km [+1.0; +6.9], an intermediate value distinct from zero.

The growth of the Mosaic lands on the frontier

Computed on the 2019–2024 increment rather than on the total stock, the centre of mass of the Mosaic's growth lies 46.5 km north of the centre of mass of visible Agriculture. By municipality, the growth of the Mosaic has a correlation of r = 0.84 with the growth of IBGE planted soy, with almost identical magnitudes (1.53 against 1.54 Mha). The spatial correlation shows coincidence of location, not identity of content.

Satellite soy, measured in the MapBiomas raster, and field soy, surveyed by IBGE, agreed with a correlation of 0.89 in latitude for 37 years. Between 2020 and 2024 they part ways for the first time. Satellite soy retreats 7.1 km south, field soy advances 8.2 km north, a gap of almost 15 km. What retreated was the reach of the label, since new soy was appearing where the Mosaic was growing and was no longer recognised as soy.

Chapter 8 · Test 3

The rise is tied to the calendar, not to the edge of the collection

MapBiomas reprocesses the full series every year as a new "collection". We reprocessed Collections 10.1 (up to 2024), 9 (up to 2023), 8 (up to 2022) and 6 (up to 2020). If the jump in the ratio were an edge artefact, with the classifier hesitating near the end of the series, it should follow the edge of each collection, not a fixed calendar year.

Collection 6 (up to 2020) Collection 8 (up to 2022) Collection 9 (up to 2023) Collection 10.1 (up to 2024)
Figure 5. Mosaic/Agriculture ratio by collection, log scale. Each line ends at its own last year, and none of them shoots up near its own end; all of them shoot up from 2021 onwards, even the one that never gets there.

The ratio explodes from 2021 onwards in every collection that reaches that year (8, 9 and 10.1) and is absent in Collection 6, which ends in 2020 and whose last values, 4.66 in 2018, 4.06 in 2019 and 2.59 in 2020, keep falling as it approaches its own edge. Nor is the final year inflated. Recounting 2023 as the terminal year of Collection 9 and as an interior year of Collection 10.1, the terminal value is the smaller one (Collection 9 = 32.5; Collection 10.1 = 34.7); gaining a year of future did not reduce the ratio. Crossing, pixel by pixel, all the Mosaic that Collection 9 saw in 2023 against what Collection 10.1 saw at the same location in 2024, the label is stable.

Only 2.5% of the pixels that Collection 9 labelled Mosaic in 2023 move to Agriculture in Collection 10.1 a year later, the same rate as the 2.4% of pixels that had already been Mosaic for more than a decade. The three findings rule out the edge-instability hypothesis. The pattern is stable, reproduced in three collections, anchored from 2021 onwards, and coincides with the real acceleration of soy measured by IBGE.

What this test does not separate

Refuting edge instability is not the same as proving a classifier artefact. A cause shared by all collections, whether a change in the Landsat sensor around 2021 or a methodology update applied retroactively, would produce the same pattern without being an edge error. This test separates edge from calendar, not classifier from landscape.

Chapter 9 · Test 4

A finer sensor does not resolve the Mosaic into cropland

If the Mosaic were edge mixing within a 30-metre pixel, a finer sensor should reveal clean cropland. MapBiomas publishes a collection based on Sentinel-2, at 10 metres and covering 2017 to 2024, which is immune to the hypothesis of a change in the Landsat input, but which inherits the same general legend and therefore does not separate "genuinely mixed landscape" from "caution in the face of recent conversion". MapBiomas itself warns against comparing years within this collection, so here it serves to measure composition in a single year. On the calibration yardstick, pure Pasture has 2% cropland at Sentinel-2 (the floor) and pure Agriculture has 78% (the ceiling). The Mosaic, at 11.5%, is only 12% of the way between pasture and cropland.

Verdict of Test 4

An independent sensor three times finer does not resolve the Mosaic into cropland. The largest piece is still Mosaic (33.5%), and the fraction labelled with confidence is pasture and vegetation, not cropland.

In the pixels closest to the label change (pasture in 2017 already labelled Mosaic in 2024), the composition shifts to 18% cropland, 31% pasture, 43% Mosaic and 8% something else, with a clear geographic gradient.

The further south, near the consolidated agricultural core, the more the mixture resembles cropland; the further north, the more it resembles pasture and vegetation, converging with the results of Tests 1 and 2 by a third independent route.

What the four tests do not separate

None of the four tests definitively separates a behaviour of the classifier from a genuinely more intense crop-livestock integration on the frontier. Both explanations remain compatible with everything that was found. What the tests allow us to say is that the artefact share is too large to be dismissed, and that any reading that ignores it is incomplete.

Chapter 10

The double yardstick: floor from the class, ceiling from the union

The direct consequence hits this very work first, because any trend reading that depends on the MapBiomas "Agriculture" class in a window touching 2020 to 2024 is no longer reliable as it stands, and the age composition of the pastures converted in that period, which was the question the investigation started from, is the most direct case. The problem lies not in the method of analysis, but in the object it measures, which changed composition midway.

Faced with an ambiguity that the evidence does not fully close, we therefore adopted a rule for the rest of the work.

The rule

Whenever a question depends on "how much became cropland" in a window touching 2020 onwards, we report an interval, not a single number. The floor is given by the Agriculture class alone, which probably undercounts the recent expansion, and the ceiling by Agriculture plus the entire Mosaic of Uses, which probably overcounts, since it includes legitimate crop-livestock integration and old Mosaic unrelated to this episode. A conclusion is only declared robust if it holds at both ends of the interval. And whenever there is a source that does not pass through the classifier (planted soy, cattle herd, rural credit, production value, all from IBGE or from the Central Bank), it is that source, not the interval, that has the final word.

The sum does not "undo" the problem, since assuming that all the Mosaic is mislabelled cropland is false (the class already existed, stable, at around 2 million hectares before 2020, and includes genuine crop-livestock integration). It answers a coarser question ("how much left pure pasture for cropland-or-mixed-use?"), robust to the label change by construction, but which mixes productive conversion with real integration.
